Can't Understand an Official Letter in Estonian or Finnish? How to Decode It Fast

Advocat AI Legal Team 16 July 2026 10 min read

A familiar scene: an envelope with a state emblem in your mailbox, dense text in Estonian or Finnish inside, and the only things you recognise are your own name and a date. The translation app produces word salad. Your Estonian colleague is on holiday. The anxiety grows with every re-read.

Take a breath. Official letters follow templates, and even without the language you can extract the three things that matter in about ten minutes: who is writing, what kind of document it is and what the deadline is. After that you know whether to act today — or whether the letter merely informs you of something.

Step 1: identify the sender from the letterhead

The agency's name sits in the top left corner or in the signature block. These are the most frequent senders — the name alone tells you roughly what the letter is about:

Estonia

  • Politsei- ja Piirivalveamet (PPA) — police and immigration: summonses, fines, residence permits. Full guide: letter from the Estonian police.
  • Maksu- ja Tolliamet (MTA) — Tax and Customs Board: declarations, tax debts, audits.
  • Kohus (Harju Maakohus, Tallinna Halduskohus…) — a court: claims, rulings, hearing summonses.
  • Kohtutäitur — bailiff: debt enforcement, account seizures.
  • Sotsiaalkindlustusamet (SKA) — Social Insurance Board: benefits, pensions.
  • Töötukassa — Unemployment Insurance Fund.

Finland

  • Maahanmuuttovirasto (Migri) — Immigration Service: permits, citizenship. Full guide: letter from Migri.
  • Verohallinto (Vero) — Tax Administration: tax cards, returns, reassessments.
  • Kela — Social Insurance Institution: benefits, reimbursements, overpayment recovery.
  • Poliisi — police: fines, summonses, licences.
  • Ulosottolaitos — Enforcement Authority: debts, wage garnishment.
  • Hallinto-oikeus / Käräjäoikeus — Administrative Court / District Court.

Step 2: find the word that names the document type

The document type is usually printed in bold above the body text, or given in the subject line. This mini-glossary covers nine letters out of ten:

  • Otsus / Päätös — a decision. The most important type: something has been decided about you, and an appeal clock is ticking.
  • Kutse / Kutsu — a summons or invitation: appear somewhere at a set time.
  • Teade / Ilmoitus — a notice: you are being informed; action may not be required.
  • Nõue, ettekirjutus / Vaatimus, kehotus — a demand or order: a specific action is expected of you.
  • Trahv, trahviteade / Sakko — a fine.
  • Meeldetuletus / Muistutus — a reminder: the last stop before enforcement.
  • Täydennyspyyntö / Selvityspyyntö (Finland) — a request for additional documents or clarification.
  • Vaie, kaebus / Oikaisuvaatimus, valitus — objection, appeal: these words in the text mean you are being told how to challenge a decision.

Step 3: find the deadline

The deadline is the most valuable piece of information in the letter. Scan for these words — a date always stands next to them:

  • Estonian: tähtaeg (deadline), hiljemalt (no later than), … jooksul (within …), e.g. 10 päeva jooksul — within 10 days.
  • Finnish: määräaika (deadline), viimeistään (at the latest), … mennessä (by …), 30 päivän kuluessa — within 30 days.

Found the date? Straight into your calendar, with a reminder a few days ahead. And note: when the deadline is "within N days", the count often starts not on the day you read the letter but on the day of official service. In Finland a regular letter is deemed received on the seventh day after posting (Administrative Procedure Act, section 59) — part of your deadline may already be gone while the envelope sat in the mailbox.

Why machine translation fails on official letters

For everyday text, Google Translate has been good for years. Bureaucratic Estonian and Finnish are a different genre: long case-ending chains, verbal nouns, double negations and terms with no direct English equivalent.

Typical traps: vaie is not a "dispute" but a specific objection procedure with its own 30-day deadline; sundraha is not "forced money" but a penalty payment for failing to appear; the Finnish … mennessä sometimes gets swallowed, turning "pay by 15 August" into "pay on 15 August". The gist looks the same — but the deadline is already lost.

Safety rule: machine translation is fine for getting the gist. Before you act on it — pay, sign, reply, or decide "this doesn't matter" — have the translation checked by a person or a service that understands the legal context, not just the words.

What happens if you ignore the letter

Both legal systems assume a delivered letter has been read. Not knowing the language stops no clock. What follows depends on the document type:

  • A decision (otsus/päätös) takes effect, and the time to appeal runs out.
  • A summons (kutse/kutsu) ignored brings the consequences of non-appearance — in criminal matters, up to being brought in.
  • A fine goes to the bailiff (kohtutäitur/ulosotto) and grows by enforcement costs.
  • A document request closes, and the case is decided without your materials — usually not in your favour.

So even the most incomprehensible letter deserves ten minutes: sender, type, deadline. Everything else can be sorted out in the days that remain.

When the state must provide an interpreter

  • Estonia, criminal proceedings: if you do not speak Estonian, an interpreter is provided (KrMS § 10). Ask for one at the very first interview.
  • Estonia, administrative matters: proceedings run in Estonian (HMS § 20); translation is normally on you.
  • Finland: in matters initiated by the authorities, the agency arranges interpretation (Administrative Procedure Act, section 26). In matters you initiated — normally not.
  • Many Estonian agencies also answer in Russian and English by phone; in Finland, Migri, Kela and Vero maintain English-language pages and service.

Frequently asked questions

Can I just run the letter through Google Translate?

For the gist — yes. For decisions — risky. Machine translation garbles bureaucratic constructions, negations and case endings, and renders terms like vaie or sundraha inconsistently. Verify separately what exactly is required and by when.

Which words mean a deadline?

Estonian: tähtaeg, hiljemalt, … jooksul. Finnish: määräaika, viimeistään, … mennessä, … kuluessa. The date next to these words goes into your calendar first.

What happens if I ignore a letter I did not understand?

Deadlines run regardless: the decision takes effect, the case is decided without you, the fine goes to enforcement. At minimum, identify the sender and the deadline — then find help with the translation.

Is the state obliged to provide an interpreter?

In Estonian criminal proceedings, yes (KrMS § 10). In Finland, in matters initiated by the authorities (Administrative Procedure Act, section 26). In matters you initiated yourself, translation is usually your own responsibility.
